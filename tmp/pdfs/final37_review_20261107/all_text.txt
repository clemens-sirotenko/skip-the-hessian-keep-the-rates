Abstract
Second-order methods are provably faster than first-order methods, and their
efficient implementations for large-scale optimization problems have attracted
significant attention. Yet, optimization problems in ML often have nonsmooth
derivatives, which makes the existing convergence rate theory of second-order
methods inapplicable. In this paper, we propose a new semismooth Newton
method (SSN) that enjoys both global convergence rates and asymptotic superlinear
convergence without requiring second-order differentiability. Crucially, our method
does not require (generalized) Hessians to be evaluated at each iteration but only
periodically, and it reuses stale Hessians otherwise (i.e., it performs lazy Hessian
updates), saving compute cost and often leading to significant speedups in time,
whilst still maintaining strong global and local convergence rate guarantees. We
develop our theory in an infinite-dimensional setting and illustrate it with numerical
experiments on matrix factorization and neural networks with Lipschitz constraints.
1 Introduction
Recent developments in second-order optimization methods showcase their superior convergence rates
compared to first-order methods [6, 53, 54] and propose efficient implementations that are scalable
enough to solve large problems arising in ML [45, 31]. The efficiency of second-order methods is
theoretically well understood for smooth problems with Lipschitz Hessians where they provably
find second-order stationary points of non-convex functions and have fast convergence rates for
minimizing convex functions. At the same time, optimization problems in ML often have nonsmooth
elements, e.g., training DNNs with ReLU, Huberized ReLU, or ReLU 2 nonlinearities, (kernel)
support vector machines [37, 77], kernel-based optimal transport problems [42], partial correlation
network estimation [40], learning problems with LASSO and other nonsmooth (group) sparsity-
inducing penalizations [68, 46, 34], square-root regression [71]. Nonsmooth derivatives naturally
appear in optimization with inequality constraints that are treated by penalization, e.g., in nonnegative
PCA [34], nonnegative matrix factorization [11], nonnegative least squares, nonparametric maximum
likelihood estimation in mixture models [79]. Moreover, several well-established losses such as Huber,
squared hinge, or the sparsemax loss [ 47] have piecewise-defined second derivatives that exhibit

jump discontinuities at certain transition points. The majority of the global results on second-order
methods are inapplicable in these situations since the Hessians are not Lipschitz.
An important property of classical Newton methods in the smooth world is their local superlinear
convergence. This property can be ported to the nonsmooth world based on the notion of semismooth
derivatives and the celebrated semismooth Newton method (SSN) [9, 63, 64, 72, 33]. This method
is widely used for ML applications as witnessed by the above-mentioned references, where the
objectives have semismooth and even γ-order/strongly semismooth derivatives. Nevertheless, there
is a gap in the theory of global rates for second-order methods in the γ-order/strongly semismooth
setting.
A drawback of Newton methods is their potentially expensive iteration that requires evaluating the
Hessian and solving a linear system. Assuming Lipschitz Hessians, it was shown in [19] that Hessian
updates can be skipped for some iterations (i.e., one can perform lazy Hessian updates) in regularized
Newton methods; such results are not known in the nonsmooth setting.
To sum up, existing second-order methods for optimization involving nonsmooth derivatives suffer
from expensive iterations and lack the full picture of theoretical convergence guarantees that covers
both global rates and local asymptotic behavior in all the semismooth and γ-order semismooth
settings. The goal of this paper is to close these gaps.

Related works.Our paper complements several strands of existing literature on second-order
methods. First, in finite dimensions, global rates are obtained in many settings, including convex and
non-convex smooth problems, see, e.g., [54, 6, 52, 49, 24, 5, 41, 48, 18, 30], with the main assumption
that the Hessian is Lipschitz. Nonsmooth problems and global rates under the umbrella of a H¨older
Hessian assumption were considered, e.g., in [28, 29, 7, 20]. These papers use global assumptions
on the Hessian and do not consider asymptotic rates under semismoothness at the solution or local
convergence.
On the other hand, classical works [ 36, 9, 63, 64, 72, 33] show including in infinite-dimensional
settings, local superlinear convergence of SSN – a generalization of the Newton method that uses gen-
eralized second derivatives instead of the Hessian for nonsmooth problems. Globalized convergence
results are also obtained, e.g., in [58, 62, 23, 76, 14, 60, 61, 39, 51, 74, 56, 25], yet the question of
global convergence rates is not answered in these papers. [1] obtain global rates, but consider neither
theγ-order semismooth setting nor allow for lazy Hessian updates.
Reusing Hessians from previous iterates dates back to Shamanskii [ 67], who established local
convergence for nonlinear systems. More recently, [19, 8] showed that modern globalized Newton
methods can reuse Hessians with only minor complexity degradation while reducing per-iteration
cost, and established global rates. However, these works rely on Lipschitz Hessian assumptions, and
it is not known whether the same can be achieved for nonsmooth problems.
Contributions.To address the outlined gaps, we propose the first SSN combining all four desiderata:
lazy Hessian updates, global convergence rates, local superlinear convergence under semismoothness,
and improved local rates under γ-order semismoothness, satisfied by many ML applications. Our
analysis is carried out in possibly infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and, in finite dimensions,
yields superlinear convergence for f∈C 2, improving existing results. Importantly,all guarantees
are achieved simultaneously by a single class- and parameter-agnostic methodwithout requiring
knowledge of the objective class or problem parameters1. We also present experiments on constrained
NN training, matrix factorization, and support vector classification.

In this section, we impose a stronger regularity assumption standard in the SSN literature; cf. [73, 3].

projections onto polyhedral sets, and spectral thresholding/projection operators (e.g., projection onto
the PSD cone) [69, 14]. We further highlight residual maps arising in kernel optimal transport as a
recent application [43] and the classical squared hinge loss in SVM.

4.4 Faster rates in the finite-dimensionalC 2 setting
Finally, we consider the finite-dimensional locally C 2 case, where the local strong convexity as-
sumption in Assumption 2 can be weakened to local convexity. While C 2-regularity is standard in
Newton methods, existing results do not yield improved asymptotic rates. Here, local C 2-smoothness
implies ηk →0 in (15), hence λk →0 by Proposition 4.1, which together with Theorem 3.4 yields
superlinear convergence. The formal statement, proved in Section D.5, is as follows.
Theorem 4.9(Main theorem, finite dimensions, C 2).Let dim(H)<∞ , Assumption 1 hold, F
satisfy the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition (10), p <1, and the sublevel set F0 be bounded. Then,
F(x k)−F ∗ →0 , xk →x ∗, and ∥F ′(xk)∥∗ →0 linearly as k→ ∞. If additionally f is locally
C 2 and locally convex aroundx ∗, andH :=∇ 2f, then the above convergences are all superlinear.

The main technical difficulties that we resolve in this paper lie in the construction of the stopping
conditions that allow us to obtain all the results in a completely class- and parameter-agnostic way.
In particular, compared to other adaptive second-order methods, we had to introduce the second
inequality in line (2) of Algorithm 1, which is crucial for obtaining the convergence of the iterates
under PL condition. Another main technical challenge was to prove the superlinear and γ-order
superlinear convergence. The high-level idea is to show that the semismoothness allows us to obtain
an analogue of (37), but with a constant L which goes to 0 with a certain rate. The next step is to show
that locally the regularization parameter λk also goes to 0 with a certain rate, which eventually gives
the desired improved rates under semismoothness. In that way, we strictly prove that our stopping
conditions allow the algorithm to fully adapt to the local geometry of the objective function. To show
the improved local rate in Theorem 4.5, we had to introduce gradient regularization with a careful
choice of the exponent p and show that this does not ruin other results like global rates. Finally, we
prove all this despite the fact that the algorithm uses “old” Hessians from the previous iterations, and
the challenge was to show that this does not ruin other results, especially the local rates.
The introduction mentions Huber, squared hinge, and sparsemax losses. All these losses are convex,
have strongly-semismooth gradients, and Assumption 1 holds, thus, Theorem 3.5 holds. Moreover, if
one adds quadratic regularization, then these regularized losses become strongly convex, and many of
our theoretical results apply.
According to Theorem 4.4, the whole convergence picture is as follows. From any starting point, the
method converges linearly to a solution x∗ thanks to the PL condition. Since the linear convergence
xk →x ∗ is guaranteed, at some point xk starts to belong to the ball around x∗ where the local strong
convexity of Assumption 2 holds. The asymptotic semismoothness Assumption 3 and DM conditions
also start to work after some iteration when xk is sufficiently close to x∗. All this implies that λk →0
and the method starts to converge superlinearly when λk is sufficiently small. In that sense, the
superlinear convergence is local and asymptotic, but the linear rate is global and nonasymptotic.
Theorem 4.8 complements the convergence picture by quantifying the order of local superlinear
convergence underγ-order semismoothness in Assumption 4.
Compared to the existing literature on the global rates for second-order methods, which require
f∈C 2,2, our results are obtained under a weaker Assumption 1, which does not even require that
f∈C 2. For example, max{a⊤x−b,0} 2 satisfies our Assumptions 1, 3 and 4 while not being
twice continuously differentiable, nor having Lipschitz Hessian. Further, existing works obtain local
superlinear convergence under Lipschitz Hessians in the vicinity of the solution. On the contrary, our
superlinear convergence results are based on the weaker (γ-order) semismoothness Assumptions 3
and 4 and these assumptions are made only at the solution, rather than in the whole vicinity.
We would like to underline that Lemma 4.3 implies that the results on superlinear convergence
in Theorem 4.2 hold also in the infinite-dimensional C 2 setting. This also means that we have a
hierarchy of results: for f∈C 1,1, we have results on linear and sublinear convergence rates, for
f∈C 2, these results asymptotically improve, and for f∈C 2,2, it is possible to obtain improved
convergence rates. The intermediate case f∈C 2 is not well explored in the literature and our paper
gives some understanding of how algorithms should work in this situation.
To simplify the presentation, we assumed local strong convexity in Assumption 2. This can be relaxed
to local convexity to prove Proposition 4.1 and to F satisfying locally the PL condition, quadratic
growth, andx ∗ being isolated to prove the inequalities in Theorems 4.2 and 4.5.
Our theoretical results are not limited to the PL setting. As stated in Theorems 4.2 and 4.5, transition
to superlinear convergence happens whenever xk →x ∗, which can be established in settings beyond
PL, e.g., under strict convexity. Importantly, all our results are proved for a single class-agnostic
method and there is no need to check in advance whether the PL condition holds or not. And even
in the cases when PL is difficult to verify, we have global rate given by Theorems 3.2 and 3.5. Our
Theorem 3.2 states that the algorithm stays on the level set and that each iteration decreases the value
of the objective. Thus, it is sufficient to assume PL on a level set.
Regarding our results in the finite-dimensional C 2 setting, the reader might wonder why we have
both Theorem 3.5 (in particular, the statement in parentheses) and Theorem 4.9. The reason is that in
the former, the PL inequality is not needed, unlike in Theorem 4.9.
Our current theory does not prescribe a way to choose the laziness parameter m. This seems to be
due to the lack of sufficient smoothness in our setting, unlike [19]. We expect though, that in some

problems, in some neighbourhood of the generated iterates, the function is actually smoother, wherein
similar optimal choices to [19] may be available.
Finally, we conclude by mentioning that this work is a first step in deriving an algorithm that possesses
accelerated local rates and takes into account that real-world problems may have Hessians that are
costly to evaluate (this is indeed the case in our neural network example of Section 5.1). There are, of
course, many additional aspects that one could do to increase the efficiency of the proposed method,
e.g., by employing such tools as sketching or other randomization techniques, quasi-Newton updates,
etc. We leave these interesting aspects for future work.

Semismooth differentiability.Let us briefly discuss semismoothness properties of the objective
in (26). For simplicity, in our experiment we use d= 1 , so the neural network Φ(·, θ) :Rn →R .
Moreover, we assume that Φ(·;θ) uses C ∞ activation functions (in our experiment we use tanh),
so that x7→ ∇2xΦ(x;θ) exists and depends smoothly on both x and θ. For a matrix A, the squared
spectral norm satisfies ∥A∥22 =λ max(A⊤A) and can be nondifferentiable when the leading singular
value has multiplicity larger than one, see [57, Theorem 2]. Following [59, 35], we therefore replace
the exact squared spectral norm by a surrogate obtained from unrolling a fixed number K∈N
of power iterations. With the regularized normalization described below, the squared norm of the
final Hessian-vector product defines a C ∞ surrogate. The remaining nonlinearity in the penalty,
t7→(max{t,0}) 2, is C 1 with a semismooth gradient; consequently, after replacing ∥∇2xΦ(x, θ)∥22
by this power-iteration surrogate, the resulting objective is C 1 with a semismooth gradient as well,
by the chain rule for semismooth functions (see, e.g., [ 73]). Using a finite number K of power
iterations amounts to optimizing a fixed surrogate objective, whose derivatives are obtained by
differentiating through the full recursion. Although this does not provide a certified approximation
error relative to the exact penalty, in this experiment the method remained stable and effective under
this approximation.

F Limitations
While our results establish a broad convergence theory for a single class-agnostic semismooth Newton
method, several limitations remain.
First, our global linear and subsequent superlinear convergence results are stated under a global
Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition. This is a standard and widely used assumption in machine
learning theory, especially in analyses of overparameterized and nonconvex models as pointed out
in the main body of the article. Nevertheless, a local PL inequality, or more generally a Kurdyka–
Łojasiewicz-type condition, would further broaden the scope of applicability. We expect that such a
local theory is possible, but the proofs are more involved; for simplicity and clarity, we present the
global PL version.
Second, our local fast-rate theory relies on Dennis–Mor´e-type conditions, and the γ-order result in
particular requires a γ-order version of such a condition. These assumptions provide useful insight
into the mechanism behind local fast convergence, especially the interaction between semismoothness,
gradient regularization, and lazy Hessian updates. However, a more intrinsic characterization of
these conditions is still needed, for instance through active-manifold or partial-smoothness structures.
Additionally, our improved local rates rely on local (strong) convexity, though it is standard in the
literature on semismooth Newton methods to assume some nondegeneracy around the solution.
Third, our theoretical analysis assumes exact solutions of the proximal Newton subproblems. As
pointed out in the experiments section, the method appears robust to different linear and least-
squares solvers, including settings where the Hessian systems are indefinite or ill-conditioned. A
detailed convergence analysis under inexact subproblem solves would be important for large-scale
implementations, but is outside the scope of the present work.
Fourth, the improved γ-order local convergence result is new and clarifies how gradient regularization
interacts with γ-order semismoothness to yield adaptively faster local rates. At present, however,
this result is proved only in the non-lazy setting m= 1 . Extending the proof to lazy-update regimes
is not merely a technical substitution: with our current proof strategy, one would need to verify a
γ-order analogue of the lazy Dennis–Mor ´e-type condition (22). Even under H ¨older continuity of

Fifth, our theoretical results quantify the number of Hessian evaluations for a fixed lazy-update period

Finally, although our experiments cover relevant machine learning problems, they remain relatively
small-scale. In particular, the neural-network example is intended as a first demonstration of semis-
mooth second-order solvers for Lipschitz-constrained training, but a more realistic imaging processing
or inverse problems setup would require parametrizing high-dimensional denoisers. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first work to rigorously demonstrate semismooth Newton-type solvers with
global convergence rates on such classes of problems. At present, however, direct applicability is
limited to small- and medium-scale settings. Extending the approach to stochastic or randomized
Newton-type methods is an important direction for making it practical in higher dimensions.
We also note that our other assumptions, such as the global minimum assumption, (strong) convex-
ity, Lipschitz derivative, semismoothness are standard for the theoretical analysis of optimization
algorithms.
